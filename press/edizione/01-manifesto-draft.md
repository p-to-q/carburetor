## page 5 (right, two paragraphs)

a phone is a clock with extras. the extras have eaten the clock. the typical handset is now a surveillance window with a battery that degrades on a schedule you cannot see, charged from a grid you cannot taste, demanding your attention because the people who designed it were paid to make it demand your attention.

we built carburetor as a phone that refuses some of this and admits the rest. it weighs more than you'd like. it asks for fuel instead of a wall outlet. it takes forty-five seconds to wake. it cannot, by design, make a voice call. it can send a message. it shows you, on a dial you can see, how much energy is left.

## page 6 (left, two paragraphs)

every part of this device is named. every part has a number. the voltmeter reads the same supercap voltage the firmware uses. the sight glass shows the same fuel volume the firmware estimates runtime from. there is no part you cannot reach. there is no number you cannot verify.

we measured everything we say. we will not say anything we have not measured. when a receipt is not in yet, the table says so.

we built three editions of mk i. the simulator costs zero dollars and runs in a browser. the maker edition is a hundred and twenty dollars and a soldering iron. the definitive edition is seven hundred and sixty dollars and a sewing shop. all three implement the same five-layer architecture. all three emit the same telemetry frames. if you cannot build a cheap version and an expensive version against the same interfaces, the interfaces are not interfaces.

(footer: `[carburetor]` · 6)

## page 7 (right, closing — ending in Q.E.D.)

a phone you refuel is not a phone from the past. it is a phone that uncouples two things — energy and computation — that should never have been welded together.

we are not arguing that fuel is better than lithium. we are arguing that fuel is *honest* about being energy in a way lithium cannot be. that you can hold a thimble of it. that you can spill it. that you can run out of it and notice.

if this sounds like a long argument for a strange object, you are reading it correctly.

## notes for the user

three knobs you might want to turn:

1. **the Smil epigraph.** i used a paraphrase of him. alternatives: a Mumford line ("the clock, not the steam engine, is the keystone..."), a Blake fragment ("what is the price of experience?"), or none at all (page 4 with only the hero line and the three-line subtitle).
2. **"surveillance window."** this is the strongest single phrase in the draft. it's correct, but it pulls the manifesto toward critique-of-tech and away from the cleaner *we-built-this-thing* register. if you want softer, replace with "a screen with a battery."
3. **the closing triad.** *p / q / arrow* is the project's signature move. it lands. but it leans on the p-to-q brand more than your last directive suggested. if you want to lean less, replace with:
   > a thimble of fuel.
   > a sentence on a screen.
   > we built the device between them.

## what i deliberately did not do

- did not write a third spread. four pages is the spec.
- did not romanticize fuel. it is dirtier than lithium, and i did not pretend otherwise.
